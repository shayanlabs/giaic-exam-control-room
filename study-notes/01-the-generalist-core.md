# 1. The generalist core

## Simple Explanation (like you're 5)

Imagine a big kitchen.

For a long time, one boss could tell 8 cooks what to make. Cooking took a long time, so that was fine.

Then someone invented magic ovens and robot helpers. Suddenly each cook can finish 5 times more food.

Now the same boss has to decide *what* food should be made for 20 cooks instead of 8. The slow part is no longer cooking. The slow part is deciding and explaining clearly what should be cooked.

That is what happened when coding agents arrived. Building software became fast. Deciding *what* to build and writing it down clearly became the new slow part. People call this the **intent bottleneck**.

The generalist core is four jobs that work together like a team:

1. The person who decides *what* the AI should do.
2. The person who builds the AI worker.
3. The person who designs the whole company that uses many AI workers.
4. The person who keeps everything running on real computers.

When one person does all four jobs inside a customer's company, the market calls that person a Forward Deployed Engineer.

## Key Concepts Unpacked

### The intent bottleneck
Old ratio: about 1 boss (product manager) for every 8 engineers.
New ratio: about 1 boss for every 20 engineers.

Why? Because agents let each engineer build much faster. The deciding part did not get faster. So deciding became the traffic jam. That is why the first role (Outcome Architect) is so important.

### Outcome Architect
This person owns the “what” and the “why.”
They do **not** build the AI.
They write a clear instruction that says:
- What the AI worker should achieve
- What “good enough” looks like
- Which workers should be built first

Think of them as the person who writes the recipe card before anyone starts cooking.

### Digital FTE Builder
“FTE” means Full-Time Equivalent — like one full-time human worker.
A Digital FTE is an AI worker that can do a job a human used to do.
This person takes the clear instruction from the Outcome Architect and actually builds the AI worker. This is the main job the book trains you for.

### AI-Native Co. Architect
This person designs the whole company that runs on many Digital FTEs.
They decide how the AI workers talk to each other, how humans stay in control, and how the company measures success.

### Cloud AI Engineer
This person keeps the AI workers running on real cloud computers day and night.
Building is only half the job. Keeping it alive and reliable is the other half.

### The 4-role pipeline
It is just a clean hand-off:
Intent → Build → Design the company → Run it in production.

## The bigger question behind this topic

Historian Yuval Noah Harari asked something teachers can't easily answer anymore: for the first time ever, nobody knows what jobs will exist in ten years — so nobody knows exactly what to teach today. The old safe answer was "learn to code." That answer is breaking, because AI is absorbing syntax-writing in real time; within a year it may write most code better than most humans.

This book's answer: don't train syntax-writers. Train the people who stand *above* the machine — the ones who decide what to build, supervise the AI workers building it, and verify what comes back. Syntax can be automated. Judgment, specification, and deployment can't — they climb the ladder as the machine climbs it. Every one of the four generalist-core roles is a seat on that ladder.

## Why would this be on the exam?
Almost every later topic lives inside one of these four seats. If you do not understand this pipeline and the intent bottleneck, the rest of the course feels like random pieces.
